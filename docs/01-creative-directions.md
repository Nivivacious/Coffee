# Phase 1. Creative directions

**A Tale of Two Cities** · a playing-card deck about Bangalore and New York

> Different cities. Familiar feelings.

Here are three creative directions, how each one measures up to the brief's creative standard, and the one I'd pick. I've also worked out the two Jokers (The Departure and The Return) at this stage, because they change what the rest of the deck has to do.

About references: the mirrored playing-card images mentioned in the brief didn't come through with the request. So everything below starts from the traditional double-headed court card, with its rotational symmetry, corner indices, framed field and central seam. If you send the references, I'll check the visual grammar against them before Phase 2.

---

## What the Jokers ask of the deck

If every card is a mirror and the Jokers are the only two without one, the deck has a plot. Fifty-one cards argue that the two cities are alike, each pairing a small, witty proof. Then the Jokers admit the argument was about a person all along, someone who has been making that case to themselves for years.

That sets the bar for each direction. The mirroring has to stay satisfying across 52 cards. The hidden sentence has to make use of the rotation itself. And the rule has to be strong enough that you feel it when the Jokers break it.

---

## Direction I. The Seam

**Central idea.** Every card has one line that both cities share.

A single unbroken contour runs across the exact centre of each card. From one end it's an edge of the Bangalore object, and from the other it's an edge of the New York object. The roof of an auto is the roof of a cab. The lip of a steel tumbler is the rim of a diner mug. The two halves are stitched together along that line rather than stacked, and that stitch is what I'd build the deck around.

**What makes it original.** Most double-headed designs mirror one figure. Some put two different figures head to head. The Seam treats the centre as a shared border, like a river two countries both claim, so the line belongs to both cities and to neither. After 51 cards it becomes the deck's signature, and you could pick out a card from the line alone.

**Storytelling mechanism.** The hidden sentence is engraved along the seam in 4.5 pt small caps and split into two clauses. The first sits on the Bangalore side of the line and reads when Bangalore is upright. The second sits on the New York side, upside down, so you only get it after you rotate the card.

No single city gives you the whole thought. You have to turn the card, which acts out the deck's idea in your hands. It also keeps the sentence hidden until someone looks closely, since at arm's length it passes for a fine ornamental rule.

**Illustration style.** Banknote and engraving vernacular: copperplate cross-hatching, guilloché borders and stipple for tone, with no fills apart from solid black in the deepest shadows. Line weights follow a fixed scale (0.15 / 0.3 / 0.6 / 1.2 pt) so all 52 cards hold together. The stock is a warm ivory. There's one accent ink, Signal Vermilion, kept for the red-suit pips and the seam, which makes the line joining the cities the only coloured line on the card.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | At the seam | Hidden sentence (turn to finish) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black ribbed Sintex tanks on every rooftop | Wooden rooftop water towers | The tank's lid and the tower's conical cap share one profile | *Every house keeps a little worry / upstairs, just in case.* |
| Majestic (Kempegowda Bus Station), numbered platform bays | Port Authority Bus Terminal, numbered gates | Two bus roofs meet nose to nose. Platform 17 turns into Gate 17 | *The ugliest building in town / is where everyone's story starts.* |
| A Kempegowda boundary tower (1537), marking where the city was meant to stop | The 1653 palisade that gave Wall Street its name | The tower's stepped finial becomes the sharpened tops of the timber wall | *Every city begins / by deciding where it ends.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** Each card is a small engraving with a secret on it, and it pays back whoever bothers to pick it up. I can picture the gift moment easily. Someone turns a card over, reads the second half of the sentence, and goes quiet for a second.

**The Joker in this direction.** It's the only card where something crosses the seam instead of meeting at it. This is my recommendation, so the Jokers are written up in full further down.

## Direction II. Ten and a Half Hours

**Central idea.** The deck is about the time difference.

Bangalore runs 9 h 30 m ahead of New York in summer and 10 h 30 m in winter. Each card pairs two things happening at the same instant. The city that's awake is drawn in black line on ivory, and the city that's asleep is drawn in reverse, ivory line cut out of a black ground, like a white-line wood engraving. Rotate the card and day becomes night.

**What makes it original.** Anyone who lives between these two cities organises their life around the gap. There's the 7 am call that's 9:30 pm for someone else, and the hour it shifts twice a year with daylight saving. It's a very specific truth, and I can't imagine a souvenir deck thinking of it.

**Storytelling mechanism.** The seam is a terminator line, the edge between day and night. Each card's index carries two tiny times (`07:00 IST · 21:30 ET`). The hidden sentence is split between the light half and the dark half.

**Illustration style.** Positive and negative engraving, close to Bewick's white-line wood engraving and Lynd Ward's woodcut novels. It's heavy and graphic and would frame beautifully. There's no accent colour, because the contrast already does that job.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | Hidden sentence |
|---|---|---|
| 07:00, first filter coffee at a darshini counter | 21:30, last call at a dive bar | *Somebody's first cup / is somebody's last round.* |
| 22:30, auto at the "one-and-a-half" night fare | 13:00, a cab stuck in midday traffic | *After ten, everything costs one and a half. / Including the call home.* |
| 18:15, dusk at the Cubbon Park bandstand | 08:45, Central Park's off-leash hour, before 9 am | *One city winds down / while the other lets the dogs out.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** It's written for people who live on both clocks: diaspora families, couples in two time zones, anyone whose phone shows a second clock. Of the three, it's the most tender.

**Where it strains.** Strict simultaneity rules out some of the best pairings. Monsoon and snowfall never happen at the same moment, and a 4 pm storm in Bangalore is 6:30 am in New York. Fifty-one half-black cards also make a heavy set. I love the idea, but I suspect it works better as a detail than as the whole system.

**The Joker in this direction.** A wristwatch with two hour hands, both of them correct.

## Direction III. A Field Guide to Two Habitats

**Central idea.** A naturalist's guide to city life, built on convergent evolution.

In biology, convergent evolution is when two unrelated species, a world apart, develop the same trait because they face the same pressures. A bat's wing and a bird's wing are the classic example. The deck's thesis, *different cities, familiar feelings*, is the same thing happening socially. So each card becomes a specimen plate, with two "species" from two habitats drawn like a 19th-century natural-history engraving, labelled, numbered and filed under the trait they share.

**What makes it original.** It gives the deck a dry, loving voice, half scientific and half poetic, like someone describing a beetle they adore. The format gets the laugh, and the field notes are where the feeling comes in.

**Storytelling mechanism.** The centre of each card is a specimen label naming the shared "genus" (for example *Genus: Belonging-to-everyone*). The hidden sentence sits in tiny italic in the label's "Behaviour" field. Each half carries a mock binomial and a habitat note.

**Illustration style.** A natural-history plate, with precise contour, stipple, the shadows of pinned specimens, typed labels and hairline callouts. Labels get a single accent of faded ochre, somewhere between turmeric and old museum card.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | Hidden sentence |
|---|---|---|
| A community street dog with a reflective collar, a name given by the whole lane, fed by four households | The bodega cat asleep on the bread | *Owned by no one. / Answers to everyone.* |
| Mallige (jasmine) strings sold by the arm's length outside a temple | Bucket flowers outside a corner deli | *Somebody is buying flowers / for no particular reason.* |
| Namma Metro token, a round plastic chip | MetroCard, now being retired, with its status given as *Extinct* | *You kept one in a drawer / long after it stopped working.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** It's the funniest of the three, and you don't need to know either city to get it. Everyone understands a field guide.

**Where it strains.** Fifty-two specimen labels could easily turn into one joke told 52 times. The format can also drift toward museum shop rather than love letter, and the scientific framing keeps the emotion at arm's length.

**The Joker in this direction.** One specimen, pinned with two pins and carrying two habitat labels.

## Evaluation

Here's how each direction scores against the brief's creative standard (● strong · ◐ partial · ○ weak):

| | I. The Seam | II. Ten and a Half Hours | III. Field Guide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognisably both cities | ● | ● | ● |
| Connection beyond resemblance | ● the shared line carries the meaning | ● | ● convergent evolution |
| Transformation feels inevitable | ● built into the system | ◐ day/night is a flip rather than a morph | ◐ the label joins them more than the drawing does |
| Hidden sentence adds a layer | ● rotation completes it | ● | ◐ reads as a caption |
| Frameable as a print | ● | ● | ● |
| Holds up across 52 cards | ● | ◐ simultaneity constrains casting | ◐ the format gets repetitive |
| Joker lands as a reveal | ● breaking the seam is felt | ◐ | ◐ |
| Readable to outsiders | ● | ◐ | ● |

## Recommendation

I'd go with Direction I, The Seam. It's the only direction where the mirroring, the hidden sentence and the Jokers all come from one idea, a single line shared by two places. It's also the strictest design system. Every card gets the same structural problem to solve, and that shared problem is what makes a deck feel like a deck rather than 52 separate illustrations.

I'd still borrow from the other two. From Ten and a Half Hours, take a tiny dual-time stamp in the corner ornament of every card (for example `IST +9:30`), used lightly and never as a rule. People who live on both clocks will spot it, and everyone else will read it as ornament.

From the Field Guide, take convergent evolution as the deck's underlying thesis. It can live in the booklet and the box copy, and it makes a good casting rule: *pair things that solved the same human problem, not things that look alike.* The resemblance is what catches the eye, and the shared problem is what the card is actually about.

## The two Jokers, The Departure and The Return

> *Funny how you can miss two places at once.*

### The pull

In a Bangalore darshini, filter coffee is pulled, poured from a height between a steel tumbler and a davara. It travels through the air in a long arc, back and forth, and that travel is what gets it right. It cools to a drinkable heat and builds its froth, and none of that dilutes it. The trip between vessels is what finishes the coffee.

The Jokers are about a person like that, someone who lives in the stream between the two cups, always on the way somewhere and better for it.

There's one more fact tucked inside this. In New York, ordering coffee "regular" gets you milk and sugar, which is exactly how Bangalore filter coffee comes by default. The two cities had already agreed on how a person should take their coffee.

### How the Jokers break the deck

Every other card has rotational symmetry. It looks the same either way up, because on that card the cities are interchangeable. The Jokers can't work that way, **because a pour only goes down.** Gravity gives these two cards an up. They're the only cards in the deck you can hold the wrong way, because a person, unlike a city, doesn't come out the same when you turn them around.

| Rule on the other 51 cards | What the Jokers do |
|---|---|
| A horizontal seam joins the two halves | The coffee stream crosses the seam diagonally. It's the only diagonal in the deck |
| Rotational symmetry: the same either way up | One way up. Turned over, the coffee would pour into the sky |
| Indices in two opposite corners | One index only, top left: a small vertical **JOKER** |
| The sentence is split across two halves | The sentence is unbroken, engraved inside the coffee stream, running in the direction of the pour |
| Each card stands alone | Each Joker is incomplete without the other. Laid side by side, they're mirror images. The pair has **reflective** symmetry, which no single card has |

The reveal is in that last row. Everywhere else the deck uses rotation, which says *it's the same either way*. The Jokers use reflection, which says *you need both to be whole*.

### The Departure

- **Top left:** a steel tumbler, tilted, high on the card, with one hard engraved highlight on its rim. It's the only object in the deck drawn at an angle.
- **Bottom right:** a paper diner cup with a Greek-key band, in the spirit of the classic New York coffee-shop cup. A meander band and an amphora silhouette suggest the original without copying it. The cup is empty and new.
- **Between them:** the pull, one long unbroken ribbon of fine parallel engraved lines arcing down across the card and straight through the Signal Vermilion seam. The seam is still there, faint and broken where the stream passes, so you can see the deck's rule and the moment it gets broken.
- **The sentence** runs in micro-caps inside the stream, starting at the tumbler's lip: FUNNY HOW YOU CAN MISS TWO PLACES AT ONCE.
- **Small details:** a crown of froth starting to form in the paper cup. There are no hands and no figure, because the stream stands in for the person.
- **Title cartouche (bottom left, in the space the missing second index leaves):** THE DEPARTURE.

### The Return

- **Top right:** the paper cup, now tilted and pouring.
- **Bottom left:** a steel davara, the wide shallow bowl, already holding a little coffee. Someone kept some for you.
- **The stream:** the same arc, mirrored horizontally, with slightly more open hatching so it reads lighter. You come back a little changed, with more milk, the way they take it over there.
- **The sentence:** the same line inside the stream, running with the pour.
- **Title cartouche (bottom right):** THE RETURN.

Put them side by side, Departure on the left and Return on the right, and the two streams form a single arch. It's the only complete symmetrical shape the Jokers make, and you only see it when both cards are on the table.

### Why this metaphor

It stays clear of every cliché on the list. There are no planes, hearts, globes or split skylines. Bangalore residents will recognise the ritual at once, and anyone else will still see two cups and a pour. It also fits the physical card. The symmetry breaks because of gravity, which gives it a reason beyond decoration. Once someone notices that every card except these two can be turned, the deck has made its argument.

## Pushing back on the starting pairings (for Phase 2)

The brief asked me to push back, so here are some early provocations on the five hero pairings. I'm not replacing any of them yet.

- **Auto × cab.** The visual match is obvious. The shared human problem is better, and it's the refusal: the auto that won't go to Whitefield, the cab that won't go to Brooklyn. A candidate sentence is *He won't go to Whitefield. / He won't go to Brooklyn.*, with a closing line in the seam ornament, *Nobody wants to go where you live.* It gets a laugh first, and then you realise it's about home.
- **Church Street × Bleecker Street.** I'd consider **St Marks Road × St Marks Place** instead. Both cities have one, and the coincidence alone makes people smile. Koshy's (since 1940) is on one side and the East Village on the other. *Same saint. / Very different evenings.*
- **Monsoon × snowfall.** Bangalore's real weather signature is the sudden 4 pm pre-monsoon thunderstorm in April and May, more than the monsoon itself. It's more specific, and every resident knows it.
- **Darshini × diner.** The connection might lie in how you take your coffee ("coffee regular") more than in the food. That overlaps with the Jokers, though, so to avoid repeating the motif the darshini card should probably centre on standing versus sitting, idli versus the plate special, or the "your usual" ritual.
- **Cubbon × Central Park.** This one works as briefed. The detail worth finding is the morning walkers, meaning the Cubbon laughing-club regulars and the Central Park loop runners.
