# Phase 1 — Creative Directions

**A Tale of Two Homes** · a playing-card deck about Bangalore and New York

> Different cities. Familiar feelings.

This document proposes three creative directions, compares them against the brief's creative standard, and recommends one. It also sets out the two Jokers (The Departure and The Return), because they change what the rest of the deck has to do.

A note on references: the mirrored playing-card images mentioned in the brief didn't come through with the request. Everything below works from the traditional double-headed court card: rotational symmetry, corner indices, a framed field, a central seam. Send the references and I'll check the visual grammar against them before Phase 2.

---

## The problem the Jokers set

The Jokers change the brief. If every card is a mirror and the Jokers are the only cards without one, the deck has a plot:

- **51 cards argue that the two cities are alike.** Each pairing is a small, witty proof.
- **The Jokers admit that the argument was never about cities.** It was about a person who has had to make it, to themselves, for years.

So each direction below has to do three things. It needs a mirroring mechanism that is satisfying 52 times without going stale. It needs a hidden-sentence system that uses the rotation, not just the space. And it needs a rule strong enough that breaking it on the Jokers is something you feel.

---

## Direction I — The Seam

**Central idea.** Every card has one line that both cities share.

Running across the exact centre of each card is a single unbroken contour. Read from one end, it's an edge of the Bangalore object. Read from the other, it's an edge of the New York object. The roof of an auto is the roof of a cab. The lip of a steel tumbler is the rim of a diner mug. The two halves aren't stacked. They're stitched together, and the stitch is the point.

**What makes it original.** Most double-headed designs mirror one figure. Some put two different figures head to head. The Seam treats the centre as a shared border, the way two countries share a river. The line belongs to both and to neither. Across 51 cards, that line becomes the deck's signature. You'd recognise a card from it alone.

**Storytelling mechanism: the sentence you have to turn the card to finish.** The hidden sentence is engraved along the seam in 4.5 pt small caps, broken into two clauses:

- The first clause sits on the Bangalore side of the line and reads when Bangalore is upright.
- The second clause sits on the New York side, upside down, and only reads once you rotate the card.

You can't read the whole thought from one city. You have to turn the card, which is the deck's idea acted out in your hands. It's also why the sentence is discovered rather than announced: at arm's length it looks like a fine ornamental rule.

**Illustration style.** Banknote and engraving vernacular. Copperplate cross-hatching, guilloché borders, stipple for tone, and no fills except solid black for deep shadow. Line weights on a fixed scale (0.15 / 0.3 / 0.6 / 1.2 pt) so all 52 cards hold together. Warm ivory stock. One accent ink, Signal Vermilion, used only for red-suit pips and the seam itself, so the line that joins the cities is the only coloured line on the card.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | At the seam | Hidden sentence (turn to finish) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black ribbed Sintex tanks on every rooftop | Wooden rooftop water towers | The tank's lid and the tower's conical cap share one profile | *Every house keeps a little worry / upstairs, just in case.* |
| Majestic (Kempegowda Bus Station), numbered platform bays | Port Authority Bus Terminal, numbered gates | Two bus roofs meet nose to nose. Platform 17 turns into Gate 17 | *The ugliest building in town / is where everyone's story starts.* |
| A Kempegowda boundary tower (1537), marking where the city was meant to stop | The 1653 palisade that gave Wall Street its name | The tower's stepped finial becomes the sharpened tops of the timber wall | *Every city begins / by deciding where it ends.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** Each card is a small engraving with a secret on it. It rewards the person who picks it up. The gift moment is easy to picture: someone turns a card over, reads the second half of the sentence, and goes quiet for a second.

**The Joker in this direction.** The only card where something crosses the seam instead of meeting at it. (Developed in full below. This is the recommendation.)

## Direction II — Ten and a Half Hours

**Central idea.** The deck is about the time difference.

Bangalore runs 9 h 30 m ahead of New York in summer and 10 h 30 m in winter. Each card pairs two things happening at the same instant. The city that's awake is drawn in black line on ivory. The city that's asleep is drawn in reverse, ivory line cut out of a black ground, like a white-line wood engraving. Rotate the card and day becomes night.

**What makes it original.** The time difference shapes the life of anyone who lives between these two cities: the 7 am call that's 9:30 pm for someone else, the gap that shifts by an hour twice a year with daylight saving. It's a very specific truth, and no souvenir deck would think of it.

**Storytelling mechanism.** The seam is a terminator line, the edge between day and night. Each card's index carries two tiny times (`07:00 IST · 21:30 ET`). The hidden sentence is split between the light half and the dark half.

**Illustration style.** Positive and negative engraving, close to Bewick's white-line wood engraving and Lynd Ward's woodcut novels. Heavy, graphic, very framable. No accent colour. The contrast does the work.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | Hidden sentence |
|---|---|---|
| 07:00 — first filter coffee at a darshini counter | 21:30 — last call at a dive bar | *Somebody's first cup / is somebody's last round.* |
| 22:30 — auto at the "one-and-a-half" night fare | 13:00 — a cab stuck in midday traffic | *After ten, everything costs one and a half. / Including the call home.* |
| 18:15 — dusk at the Cubbon Park bandstand | 08:45 — Central Park's off-leash hour, before 9 am | *One city winds down / while the other lets the dogs out.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** It's written for the people who live on both clocks: diaspora families, couples in two time zones, anyone whose phone shows a second clock. It's the most tender of the three directions.

**Where it strains.** Strict simultaneity rules out some of the best pairings. Monsoon and snowfall are never simultaneous, and a 4 pm storm in Bangalore is 6:30 am in New York. Fifty-one half-black cards are also heavy as a set. The idea is very strong, but it may be stronger as a detail than as the whole system.

**The Joker in this direction.** A wristwatch with two hour hands, both of them correct.

## Direction III — A Field Guide to Two Habitats

**Central idea.** A naturalist's guide to city life, built on convergent evolution.

In biology, convergent evolution is when two unrelated species, a world apart, develop the same trait because they face the same pressures. A bat's wing and a bird's wing. The deck's thesis, *different cities, familiar feelings*, is that phenomenon in social form. So each card is a specimen plate. Two "species" from two habitats, drawn like a 19th-century natural-history engraving, labelled, numbered, and filed under the shared trait that made them alike.

**What makes it original.** It gives the deck a scientific-poetic voice, dry and loving, the register of someone describing a beetle they adore. The joke is the format. The feeling arrives in the field notes.

**Storytelling mechanism.** The centre of each card is a specimen label giving the shared "genus" (for example *Genus: Belonging-to-everyone*). The hidden sentence sits in the label's "Behaviour" field in tiny italic. Each half carries a mock binomial and a habitat note.

**Illustration style.** Natural-history plate: precise contour, stipple, pinned-specimen shadows, typed labels, hairline callouts. A single accent of faded ochre (turmeric or old museum card) for labels only.

**Three example pairings**

| Bangalore | New York | Hidden sentence |
|---|---|---|
| A community street dog: reflective collar, a name given by the whole lane, fed by four households | The bodega cat asleep on the bread | *Owned by no one. / Answers to everyone.* |
| Mallige (jasmine) strings sold by the arm's length outside a temple | Bucket flowers outside a corner deli | *Somebody is buying flowers / for no particular reason.* |
| Namma Metro token, a round plastic chip | MetroCard, now being retired. Status: *Extinct* | *You kept one in a drawer / long after it stopped working.* |

**Why someone would own or gift it.** It's the funniest of the three directions and the easiest to understand without knowing either city. Anyone can enjoy a field guide.

**Where it strains.** Fifty-two specimen labels risk becoming a single joke. The format can feel more like a museum shop than a love letter, and it holds emotion at a slight scientific distance.

**The Joker in this direction.** One specimen, pinned with two pins and carrying two habitat labels.

## Recommendation: Direction I, The Seam

The Seam is the only direction where the mirroring mechanism, the hidden sentence and the Jokers are all the same idea: **one line shared by two places.** It's also the most rigorous design system. It gives every card the same structural problem to solve, and that's what makes a deck feel cohesive instead of like 52 separate illustrations.

It should take the best idea from each of the other two:

- **From Ten and a Half Hours:** a micro dual-time stamp in the corner ornament of every card, as a whisper rather than a rule (for example `IST +9:30`). People who live on both clocks will notice it. Everyone else will read it as ornament.
- **From the Field Guide:** convergent evolution as the deck's quiet thesis, used in the booklet and box copy, and as a casting rule: *pair things that solved the same human problem, not things that look alike.* The look-alike is the hook. The shared problem is the meaning.

## The Jokers: The Departure and The Return

> *Funny how you can miss two places at once.*

### The metaphor: the pull

In a Bangalore darshini, filter coffee is pulled, poured from a height between a steel tumbler and a davara. The coffee travels through the air in a long arc, back and forth, and the travelling is what makes it right. It cools to a drinkable heat and gains its froth. It isn't diluted by moving between vessels. It's finished by it.

That's the person the Jokers are about. Not one cup or the other, but the stream in between: always going somewhere, and better for it.

One more fact hides inside this. In New York, ordering coffee "regular" means milk and sugar, which is exactly how Bangalore filter coffee comes by default. The two cities had already agreed on how a person should take their coffee.

### How the Jokers break the deck

Every other card has rotational symmetry. It looks the same either way up, because the cities are interchangeable on it. The Jokers can't work that way, **because a pour only goes down.** Gravity gives these two cards an up. They're the only cards in the deck that can be held the wrong way, because a person, unlike a city, isn't reversible.

| Rule on the other 51 cards | What the Jokers do |
|---|---|
| A horizontal seam joins the two halves | The coffee stream crosses the seam diagonally. It's the only diagonal in the deck |
| Rotational symmetry: the same either way up | One way up. Turned over, the coffee would pour into the sky |
| Indices in two opposite corners | One index only, top left: a small vertical **JOKER** |
| The sentence is split across two halves | The sentence is unbroken, engraved inside the coffee stream, running in the direction of the pour |
| Each card stands alone | Each Joker is incomplete without the other. Laid side by side, they're mirror images. The pair has **reflective** symmetry, which no single card has |

The last row carries the reveal. The deck uses rotation everywhere, which means *it's the same either way*. The Jokers use reflection, which means *you need both to be whole*.

### The Departure

- **Top left:** a steel tumbler, tilted, high on the card, its rim catching one hard engraved highlight. It's the only object in the deck drawn at an angle.
- **Bottom right:** a Greek-key paper diner cup in the spirit of the classic New York coffee-shop cup, evoked with a meander band and an amphora silhouette rather than copied. It's empty and new.
- **Between them:** the pull, one long unbroken ribbon of fine parallel engraved lines arcing down across the card and straight through the Signal Vermilion seam. The seam is still there, faint and interrupted where the stream passes. The deck's rule is visible, and so is the moment it's broken.
- **The sentence** runs in micro-caps inside the stream, starting at the tumbler's lip: FUNNY HOW YOU CAN MISS TWO PLACES AT ONCE.
- **Small details:** a crown of froth beginning to form in the paper cup. No hands and no figure. The person is the stream.
- **Title cartouche (bottom left, in the space the missing second index leaves):** THE DEPARTURE.

### The Return

- **Top right:** the paper cup, now tilted and pouring.
- **Bottom left:** a steel davara, the wide shallow bowl, which already holds a little coffee. Someone kept some for you.
- **The stream:** the same arc, horizontally mirrored, drawn with slightly more open hatching so it's a lighter tone. You come back a little changed: more milk, the way they take it over there.
- **The sentence:** the same line inside the stream, running with the pour.
- **Title cartouche (bottom right):** THE RETURN.

Side by side, Departure on the left and Return on the right, the two streams form a single arch. It's the only complete symmetrical shape the Jokers make, and it only exists when both cards are on the table.

### Why this metaphor

It avoids every cliché on the list: no planes, hearts, globes or split skylines. It's rooted in a ritual that Bangalore residents will recognise immediately, and it reads to anyone as two cups and a pour. It's also physically true to the card. The symmetry breaks for a reason, gravity, not for decoration. Once someone notices that every card but these two turns, the deck has made its argument.

## Challenges to the starting pairings (for Phase 2)

The brief asked me to push back. Here are early provocations on the five hero pairings, without replacing them yet:

- **Auto × cab.** The look-alike is obvious. The shared human problem is better: the refusal. The auto that won't go to Whitefield, the cab that won't go to Brooklyn. A candidate sentence: *He won't go to Whitefield. / He won't go to Brooklyn.* with a closing line in the seam ornament: *Nobody wants to go where you live.* It's funny first, then quietly about home.
- **Church Street × Bleecker Street.** Consider **St Marks Road × St Marks Place**. Both cities have one, and the coincidence makes people smile on its own. Koshy's (since 1940) on one side, the East Village on the other. *Same saint. / Very different evenings.*
- **Monsoon × snowfall.** Bangalore's weather signature is less the monsoon than the sudden 4 pm pre-monsoon thunderstorm in April and May. That's more specific, and any resident will recognise it.
- **Darshini × diner.** The connection may be how you take it, not the food: "coffee regular". This overlaps with the Jokers, so the darshini card should probably centre on standing versus sitting, idli and the plate special, or the "your usual" ritual, to avoid repeating the motif.
- **Cubbon × Central Park.** This one works as briefed. The specific detail to find is the morning walkers, the Cubbon laughing-club regulars and the Central Park loop runners.
